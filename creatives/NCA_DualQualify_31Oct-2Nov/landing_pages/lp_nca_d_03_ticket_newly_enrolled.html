Canada sounds big. Your first year already feels full

You are learning court, drafting for a senior and earning very little. Canada keeps coming up, from friends abroad and from posts you scroll past at night. But it sounds like a decision with a lot of zeros in it.

So you do what most juniors do. You save the posts, you tell yourself you will look at it properly one day, and the day does not come.

What you want: to find out if it is for you, cheaply

You want to find out whether the Canada route is right for you before you spend real money or time on it.

Failed alternative 1: deciding before you have tried

Some juniors decide 'not for me' without ever seeing the route. Others decide 'yes' on a friend's word. Both are guesses. Three evenings of trying the real thing turns the guess into an answer.

Failed alternative 2: buying a course before you know the route

Paying for a full prep course or a foreign degree first is the expensive way to learn whether you even like the material. Try it before you buy anything large.

Seniors give good advice about Indian practice. Very few of them have taken the NCA route, so on Canada their advice is a guess too.

The people who can tell you what the route is really like are those who have walked it, and the bootcamp brings that experience into one room for three days.

The problem is not commitment. It is information

You do not lack commitment. You lack information, and information is cheap to get. Once you have it, your position changes: from a junior wondering about Canada to a junior with a tested plan.

What not trying costs

Not trying costs you the easiest years to start. Early evenings are the most flexible you will have, and the route rewards steady preparation started early.

It also costs you the comparison. Without trying, you cannot tell whether your real interest is Canada, another country or staying in India. The trial answers that too.

What the route looks like, in 3 steps

  1. NCA exams, from India. About 2 hours a day on the core subjects, in our bootcamp's plan, written online from home.
  2. Small Canadian work while you prepare. Research and drafting support for Canadian lawyers and startups, found on LinkedIn and Upwork.
  3. Bar licensing, then practise in Canada. With the Certificate of Qualification, you take your province's licensing step.
