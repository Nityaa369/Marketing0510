01

Heavy cases that rarely end quickly

Your matters are divorces, maintenance, custody and domestic disputes. Clients bring you their hardest years, hearings stretch on, and every adjournment lands on someone who is already exhausted.

More and more of the evidence lives on phones: chats, shared locations, photos, matrimonial site profiles. You have watched private information travel in ways your clients never imagined when they tapped agree.

You care about the people you represent. But the work is emotionally draining, the calls come at all hours, and it rarely leads towards a different kind of practice.

02

Document work with a different rhythm

Every consumer app runs on user data, user content and user payments. Tech companies need lawyers to draft their privacy policies, terms and contracts, and to say clearly what they can and cannot promise the people who use them.

A tech law career gives you that document-based work with startups and tech companies, done at your desk and at your pace. Your family practice continues, and you add a field where the client is a business, not a family in crisis.

04

You already understand privacy, in practice

Privacy work is about the promises an app makes to people: what it collects, who sees it, and what happens when something goes wrong. You have seen what happens to real families when those promises break.

That lived understanding is an asset, not a detour. The problem is that tech companies do not think of a family lawyer when they need privacy help, simply because nobody has positioned you that way yet. One careful privacy review, shown to a founder, starts to change that.

If the next years look like the last

Staying unclear means carrying the same emotional load with no second path growing beside it. A gentle start now, an hour or two a day, keeps a calmer future open without asking you to drop anyone. Nothing about your current clients has to change.

What tech law work looks like for a family and matrimonial lawyer

Checking whether an app's terms link to a compliant privacy policy, one of the six checks in Day 3's terms review. You would read it thinking of the person whose chats and photos sit on that server. You know which details people later regret sharing, which makes your review practical.

Learning how consumer apps make money. Day 2 covers six business models, starting with advertising and subscription. Social and dating apps often use these models, and each one creates its own privacy and contract work.

Building professional relationships with care. Advocates may not solicit work, so the outreach we teach starts with something useful: an observation about a public policy, offered to a founder or a lawyer abroad, never a request for a brief.
