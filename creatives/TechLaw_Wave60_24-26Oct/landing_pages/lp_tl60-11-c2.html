01

Notice, complaint, date, repeat

Your week runs on Section 138. A client brings a dishonoured cheque, you check the dates, draft the demand notice and file the complaint once the notice period runs out.

You do this well. You can spot a defective notice in seconds: a wrong amount, a missed deadline, a demand that never reached the drawer. That precision is why clients come back.

But the matters look alike, the dates stretch on, and the work rarely leads anywhere new. You are known as the cheque bounce lawyer, and that label is hard to grow out of.

02

The same eye for gaps, a different client

Tech law is the legal work that technology companies need done: their contracts, privacy, compliance and disputes. A startup's terms of use, a software development agreement and a refund policy all fail when something is missing.

Moving into tech law means the documents on your desk come from founders and product teams, not from dishonoured cheques. You keep your practice and add a second career built on the habit of checking every line. The client changes from someone chasing a debt to a company that wants to launch without holes in its paperwork.

04

Your notice drafting is the asset

A demand notice is a checklist in disguise. Was the cheque presented in time? Did the notice go out within the period? Is the demand clear? You test every document against what the law requires.

Reviewing a startup's terms works the same way, and on Day 3 we check terms against six points. Your problem is not effort or ability. Nobody outside your court sees the skill, because it only ever meets cheques.

What waiting costs

If nothing changes, next year probably looks like this one: the same notices, the same dates, the same label. That is a decent practice. It is simply a smaller career than your drafting could support.

05

What tech law work looks like for a cheque bounce lawyer

Reviewing a startup's terms of use. You read them the way you read a notice: does the company exclude liability for free services, cap it at the fee for paid ones, and say clearly how either side can end things?

Drafting the payment side of a software development agreement. Milestones, consideration and acceptance testing decide when a developer gets paid. You already know what vague payment terms lead to, because your clients live with the result.

Building relationships with founders and foreign lawyers. Bar Council rules restrict advertising and soliciting, so the outreach we show starts with a useful observation about a public document. It is never a request for a brief. On Day 1 we also show how lawyers network with US lawyers by helping first, through blogs, podcasts and posts.
