01

You write all day, and none of it uses your law degree

You are good with words. Clients send briefs, you turn them around fast, and the work goes out under their name. Payment is by the word or the piece, and the rate rarely moves.

Your LLB sits behind all of it. You may even write about law, explaining judgments for a firm's blog. But you are hired as a writer, so you are paid and treated as one.

02

Writing that carries legal weight

Terms of use, privacy policies, refund policies and influencer agreements are all writing. They must be clear enough for a customer and precise enough to hold up in a dispute. That combination is rare, and tech companies need it on every website and app they launch.

Moving into tech law, the legal side of technology businesses, would make you the person who drafts those documents as a lawyer. Your writing stays your strength, but it is paid for as legal work, under your own name.

You have the hard half already

Many lawyers find plain writing difficult. Their policies read like a judgment, and founders do not understand them. You can write for a reader, so the positioning you are missing is simple: not a writer with an LLB, but a lawyer who writes clearly.

Writing is honest work, and you are not doing anything wrong by staying. The cost is only that your best skill keeps being priced as content, and the law degree keeps waiting. Three evenings is a small price to see the other option clearly.

05

What tech law work looks like for an LLB holder who writes content

Website policies are the obvious start. Every startup site needs terms of use, a privacy policy and a cookie policy. On Day 3 the camp reviews a startup's terms against six checks, such as liability caps, termination and IP ownership.

E-commerce gives you more. A refund policy in the camp's 4 parts covers when a buyer can cancel, when they can return, how the return works, and how and when the money goes back. It is legal drafting that customers actually read.

Creators are a natural fit too. Day 3 covers legal work for influencers and a 5-clause influencer agreement: engagement and fee, deliverables, IP licence, content ownership and brand guidelines. You know how content deals work from the creator's side, including where fees and usage rights usually cause arguments.
