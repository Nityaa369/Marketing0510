01

You can draft almost anything. Tech clients still go elsewhere.

Share purchase agreements, shareholder agreements, leases, supply contracts, service agreements. You know how a definitions clause can save a deal and how a weak indemnity can sink one.

Yet when a software company needs a contract, it usually goes to a firm's technology team or a lawyer who calls themselves a tech lawyer. Your drafting is as good. Your label is not the one they search for.

When you open a SaaS agreement or a statement of work, the structure looks familiar. Then the schedules talk about uptime, service credits and source code escrow, and you are not sure which risks matter most.

02

Drafting for the companies that buy contracts every week

Tech law is the legal work that technology companies need, and contracts are the largest share of it, alongside privacy, compliance and disputes. For a drafting lawyer, the move is simple to describe. You become the lawyer tech companies hire for their contracts, at a firm tech team, inside a startup, or as an independent practice.

Tech companies sign a steady stream of documents: master service agreements, statements of work, change orders, SLAs, licence terms, reseller and co-branding agreements. Each one is drafting work you already know how to do well.

04

Your craft travels. The industry vocabulary is what is missing.

A software development agreement is still a contract. Offer, consideration, warranties and termination all behave the way you know. What changes is a short list of tech concepts that decide where the risk sits.

So this is not about drafting harder or faster. It is about pointing the skill you already have at an industry that needs it, and naming yourself in words that industry recognises. A founder who reads 'I draft software development agreements and SaaS terms' knows at once that you can help. A founder who reads 'corporate and commercial lawyer' has to guess.

The cost of staying a generalist drafter

You can keep drafting well for clients who find you through referrals, and that is a fine practice. The only cost is that the companies signing the most contracts keep looking past a profile that does not mention their work.

05

What tech law work looks like for a contract drafting lawyer

A typical first task is a software development agreement for a startup that is hiring a development agency. You fix the milestones, tie payment to them, set acceptance testing, and make sure the IP in the code passes to the client on handover.

Next come the recurring documents: master service agreements with statements of work under them, change orders when the scope moves, and SLAs that promise uptime and pay credits when the service fails. White label and reseller agreements follow the same logic.

As you grow, you draft the user-facing side too: terms of use and privacy policies. These are contracts written for millions of readers, so your habit of plain, tight drafting matters even more.
