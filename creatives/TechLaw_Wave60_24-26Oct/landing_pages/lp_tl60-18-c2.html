01

Always called after publication

A post goes viral, a video names someone, a channel runs a story. Then your phone rings. You send the notice, draft the reply or file the suit, and argue about words that are already out in the world.

You understand reputation, speech and content better than almost any other lawyer. You know how one careless line in a caption can turn into a year of litigation. You also know which words are safe and which ones invite a notice.

Yet the work only begins once the damage is public. The creators and brands making content every day rarely think of you until it is too late to help them cheaply.

02

Get in before the content goes out

Creators, brands and platforms run on content contracts: influencer agreements, licences, community standards and terms of use. Tech law includes all of this, and the work happens before anything is posted.

A tech law career gives you that drafting and advisory work with creators and platforms, while your media practice continues. It is the same understanding of content, used at the start of the story instead of the end. Your clients become the people producing content, not only the people hurt by it.

03

Routes media lawyers usually try

Taking on more defamation mattersThe caseload grows, but each file is still a response to harm already done. It deepens your current practice rather than building a new career.
Writing columns and commentary on media lawIt raises your profile among other lawyers, but creators and founders rarely read it. Visibility without a document they need seldom turns into work.
Seeking retainers with media housesThose seats are few and usually filled through long relationships. Waiting for one leaves your skill parked for years. Meanwhile, new creators start out every day with nobody advising them.
04

Content is a contract question

Who owns a video, who may reuse it, what a brand can demand and what a creator can refuse are all contract terms. You already weigh these questions every time you defend a piece of content. Creators often sign brand deals without anyone checking those terms.

So the gap is positioning, not ability. Creators and platforms simply do not know that a defamation lawyer can draft their agreements. One clean influencer agreement is often enough to show them. Creators judge a lawyer by the agreement in front of them.

If you keep waiting for the call

If you stay unclear, your work keeps starting after the harm, and the growing world of creators and platforms keeps hiring other people at the stage where you could help most. That stage is still open to you.

Reviewing a platform's terms, including its community standards and IP ownership, two of the six checks in Day 3's review. You know exactly how vague standards play out once a post is challenged. Your review would flag them before the first takedown request arrives.

Advising ad-funded platforms. Day 2 explains six ways tech companies make money, including advertising, and each creates content, IP and policy work. Tech law also covers disputes over impersonation, defamation and takedowns, where your court experience counts.
