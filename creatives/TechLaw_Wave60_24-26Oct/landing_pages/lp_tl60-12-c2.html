01

You always arrive after the money is gone

A client calls in a panic. A video call from a fake officer, a payment link, a screen-sharing app, and the savings are gone. You file the complaint, chase the bank and the police, and push for the money to be traced.

Over time you have learned more about wallets, payment links and app permissions than most lawyers ever will. You can point to the exact step where a transaction could have been stopped. You also know how victims think, and which warnings they scrolled past.

Yet the work always starts after the harm. Each case is urgent and painful, and the knowledge you have built stays locked inside fraud files that nobody else reads.

02

Use what you know before anything goes wrong

Tech companies need lawyers who understand how online money and user accounts actually move. They need that thinking in their terms of use, their payment flows and their contracts, long before any dispute reaches a police station. Few founders have anyone on the team who has watched a scam unfold from the victim's side.

A tech law career puts you on that side of the product. Your practice continues, and a second line of work opens with fintech apps, platforms and startups that build the very tools your clients use.

04

Timing is the problem, not skill

You already think like a product reviewer. When you trace a fraud, you ask who allowed what, at which step, under which terms. That is the same question a tech lawyer asks while a product is being built.

So the shift is one of positioning. The same understanding, placed before launch instead of after a loss, is what fintech and platform companies want from a lawyer. You do not need to work harder to get there.

If the knowledge stays in fraud files

Staying where you are keeps you useful to people on their worst day, and that matters. But the understanding you have built may never reach the companies that could use it every week, and that is a quiet waste of a rare skill.

05

What tech law work looks like for a cyber fraud lawyer

Reading a payment app's terms of use and checking whether they link to a compliant privacy policy, set out community standards and explain clearly how the arrangement with a user can be ended. You would read every line asking what a confused user would do next.

Making sense of tech concepts in contracts. On Day 1 we cover APIs and SLAs, including uptime and service credits. You already know what happens when a connected service fails, so these clauses will feel practical, not abstract.

Meeting founders and lawyers abroad in the right way. Advocates may not solicit work, so the method we teach is to share a useful observation about a public document and let a professional relationship grow from there.
