01

You teach the law. You rarely get to use it.

You teach contract law, the IT Act or IPR from a syllabus, set papers, mark scripts and guide dissertations. You explain doctrine clearly, and students trust you.

Then a final-year student asks how to get into tech law, and the honest answer is that you have not done that work yourself. Your examples come from textbooks and judgments, not from a contract you drafted last month.

You may also miss practice. The timetable, committees and research targets leave little room, and going back to court full-time is not the life you chose.

02

A tech law practice that fits a college timetable

Tech law is the legal work tech companies need, which is mostly contracts, privacy, compliance and disputes. Much of it is drafting done on a laptop, one task at a time. That makes it a practice a teacher could build around lectures and holidays.

Teaching stays your main role if you want it to. What you add is a live practice: startups, foreign lawyers or firm tech teams who send you work, and a stock of real examples for your classroom. Students notice when their teacher has done the work, and your guidance on tech law careers would come from your own practice.

03

How law teachers usually try to stay close to practice

Writing journal articles on tech lawPapers build an academic record. They do not give you a client, a draft or the feel of what a founder actually asks.
Attending faculty development programmesThey update what you know about the law. They rarely put a document in front of you that a business will use.
Planning a PhD or LLM in a tech topicIt deepens theory over years. It does not build the practical drafting record that tech clients look for.
04

You already have the hard part: you can explain

Tech law clients are founders who are not lawyers. They need someone who can read a clause, find the gap and explain it in plain words. That is what you do in every lecture.

So the gap is not ability or effort. It is that you are positioned as an academic, not as a practitioner. A few finished documents and a clear profile move you from one to the other. Your habit of breaking a topic into clear steps also makes you quick at learning the tech concepts.

If practice stays on the wish list

Your teaching career is secure and worthwhile. The gentle cost is that each batch of students asks about tech law careers, and you keep answering from the outside of a field you could be working in.

05

What tech law work looks like for a law teacher or lecturer

The most natural first task is a terms review. You read a startup's terms of use against a short list of checks, mark what is missing, and send the founder a clear note on the fix. It is close to marking an answer script, with higher stakes.

Policy work suits academics well. Tech companies and think tanks need research, whitepapers and responses to draft laws, and the script names policy advocacy as one of the kinds of work tech companies need.

You could also help foreign lawyers build their profile, by writing blogs or getting them onto podcasts, which Day 3 teaches. Over time you would draft contracts and privacy policies too, and bring each one back into class. Each document you finish becomes a case study your students will remember far longer than a hypothetical.
