01

Every opening asks for experience you cannot get

Your final year is a pile of exams, a dissertation and internship applications. Most of the emails go unanswered. The internships you do get are unpaid and mostly research.

Then you read openings for freshers, and they want prior experience. You cannot get experience without a job, and you cannot get a job without experience. It feels like a closed loop.

Graduation is close, and you still do not know what you will be doing the month after it. Your parents ask, your batchmates ask, and you have no clear answer yet.

02

Graduate with tech law work already done

Tech law is what lawyers do for tech companies: draft their contracts, write their policies, keep them compliant and handle their disputes. Startups need small, finished documents, such as a privacy policy or a terms review, and they need them now.

Start tech law work in your final year and you could graduate with documents real companies have used. That changes the first interview. Instead of saying you are interested in tech law, you show what you have already done in it.

04

One real document breaks the loop

The no-experience loop has nothing to do with your effort. You have worked hard for years. The loop exists because everything you have written so far was written for examiners, not for clients.

Change the reader and the loop opens. One finished privacy policy for a real company counts as experience. So does a reviewed set of startup terms. Your law school training covers the law; the camp shows you the structure of these documents and how to reach companies that need them.

What waiting until graduation costs

If you wait until after graduation to start, you will still find your way. You will simply begin the search with the same blank experience section as most of your batch. Starting in the final year gives you a head start for a couple of hours a day.

05

What tech law work looks like for a final-year law student

Your first tasks are small and close to what you study. Checking a startup's terms for a missing liability cap or a vague termination clause draws on your contract law. Building a refund policy for an online store draws on consumer protection.

Next, you learn how tech businesses earn. Day 2 explains six models, from advertising and subscriptions to marketplaces and tech services. Each model tells you which documents a company needs and where its legal gaps tend to sit. A subscription app and a marketplace need very different terms.

You also learn to reach people. The camp shows how to find small startups in India and abroad and send a short, specific note about their terms. For a student, a reply to that note could turn into a first paid task.
