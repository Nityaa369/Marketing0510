01

The college name gets read before your work does

You studied the same Contract Act, the same CPC and the same evidence law as everyone else. You may have topped your batch. But on campus hiring lists and firm careers pages, your college never appears.

You have sent cold emails and heard nothing back. When someone does reply, the first question is where you studied, not what you can do. You have started to wonder whether a non-NLU degree caps a career before it begins.

It does not have to. It depends on where you take that degree next.

02

A field where the draft speaks first

Tech law means the contracts, privacy work, compliance and disputes that technology businesses need handled. Much of that work reaches the client as a document: a contract, a policy, a marked-up set of terms. A founder reading your redline sees whether the liability clause is fixed, and your college appears nowhere on the page.

Move into tech law and you build a career on finished work that clients can see for themselves. That is a fairer test than the one you have been sitting, and it is one you can prepare for.

04

Your college is a filter only when nobody can see your work

Recruiters lean on college names as a shortcut because they have nothing else to go on. A CV says what you studied. It does not show what you can draft.

Change what they have to go on. Bring a reviewed set of startup terms, a software development agreement, a refund policy. Now the shortcut is not needed, and your training in reading statutes and contracts closely becomes the thing being judged.

What the old contest costs you

Staying in the contest of college names is tiring. You keep applying where the filter is set against you, and every silence starts to feel personal. Choosing a field where the work is judged directly is a kinder use of the same energy.

05

What tech law work looks like for an LLB graduate from any law college

You start by reading. A startup's terms of use, a SaaS company's service levels, an app's privacy policy. You check each one against what the law and the business need, which is exactly the close reading your LLB trained.

Then you write. You draft clauses for a software development agreement, a cancellation and refund policy for an online store, an influencer agreement for a brand. Each is a plain document a business owner can put to use the same day.

Then you reach out. The camp shows how lawyers find small startups in the US, UK, Canada, Europe and India, and send a short note about a gap in their terms. The note leads with the work, so the reader never needs to ask about your college.

What you will have done by Monday night

On Day 3 you mark up a startup's terms with the hosts. You check six things: liability for free services, a cap for paid ones, clear termination, community standards, a privacy policy link and IP ownership. Then you put the gaps into a short pitch to the founder. That marked-up document is the first thing you can show anyone, and there is no college line on it.

The 6-month plan asks for 2 hours a day, split between learning and outreach. Done steadily, it could leave you with a portfolio of tech documents, a LinkedIn presence built on posts about real clauses, and early testimonials from founders. None of it is guaranteed, but every part of it is judged on the work.
