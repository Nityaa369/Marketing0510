General work every day, and no specialty to show

Your firm takes whatever comes in: a lease, a recovery notice, a property dispute, a shareholder agreement. You draft all of it, and you have become good at turning work around fast.

But when you read openings in firm tech teams or in-house legal teams at tech companies, they ask for tech contracts you have drafted. SaaS agreements. Privacy policies. Software development agreements. Your small firm rarely sees any of those.

You have the drafting habit. What is missing is the right documents in your folder.

04

Teams hire by what you can show

You are not short of skill. You draft every day, often with less support than associates at large firms get. The trouble is that your samples are in the wrong document types for the move you want.

That is a positioning gap, and a few targeted drafts close it. Your daily drafting is the asset. Point it at software development agreements, service level terms and startup terms, and your folder starts to match the roles you want.

What standing still costs

Staying a generalist is comfortable, and you will keep learning at your firm. The only cost is that the specialty you want stays on a wish list. Building a few tech samples now keeps that door open without asking you to leave anything yet.

05

What tech law work looks like for a junior associate at a small firm

In a firm tech team, your day is contracts. A client's SaaS agreement needs service level terms covering uptime, downtime and service credits. A developer contract needs clear acceptance testing and IP assignment. You already know how to mark up a contract; this gives you the terms that matter.

In-house at a tech company, the work follows the business model. On Day 2 the camp breaks down how tech companies earn, through ads, subscriptions, marketplaces, hardware and more, and what legal work each model throws up. Knowing the model makes you useful early.

You also learn the concepts that trip up most generalists. Source code versus object code decides what a client really receives. White labelling decides whose brand goes on the product. APIs decide what data moves between systems, and who answers for it.
