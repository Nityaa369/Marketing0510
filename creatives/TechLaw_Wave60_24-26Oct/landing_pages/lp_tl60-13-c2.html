01

Every complaint is a policy that failed

Your files are full of refunds that never came, orders cancelled without reason and returns the seller refused. You draft the complaint, wait through the hearings and argue over what the website promised. Sometimes you win, and the same store does it again to the next buyer.

You read more e-commerce policies than most corporate lawyers do. You can see in minutes where a cancellation clause is vague or where a refund promise has no timeline at all.

But the process is slow, the stakes per file are often modest, and you only ever meet these policies after they have failed somebody.

02

Write the policy, not the complaint

Online stores and marketplaces need refund policies, terms of use and seller arrangements drafted by someone who knows how buyers actually complain. That is consumer law applied at the drafting stage, and it is a core part of tech law.

Moving into tech law gives you a second career with e-commerce brands and startups as clients. Your forum practice stays. You add work that starts before any dispute exists, on documents that shape how a whole store treats its buyers.

What consumer lawyers usually try

Taking on more complaintsThe diary fills, but every matter still starts after the damage. It deepens the practice you have instead of opening a new one.
Appearing for companies as the opposite partyThat moves you to the other side of the table, yet the work is still a hearing. You face a different direction from the same chair.
Reading the e-commerce rules on your ownKnowing the rules helps, but a founder hires the lawyer who can show a finished policy. Reading alone leaves you with nothing to put in front of them.
04

Your consumer law already is e-commerce law

You know the buyer's side in detail: what they were promised, what they understood and where the wording let them down. That is exactly the knowledge needed to write a policy that holds up.

So nothing is missing in your skill. The gap is that founders do not know a consumer lawyer can draft their policies. One finished refund policy, shown to the right person, starts to change that. Your forum years then read as expertise, not as a separate world.

Staying on one side of the document

If you stay unclear, the policies keep failing and you keep meeting them in complaints. Your knowledge stays valuable to one client at a time, when it could shape how entire online stores treat every buyer. A small, steady start keeps that wider role within reach.

05

What tech law work looks like for a consumer forum lawyer

Drafting an e-commerce refund policy. On Day 3 we build one in four parts: when a buyer can cancel, when they can return, how the return works, and the mode and timing of the refund.

Reviewing a startup's terms. You check whether liability is capped sensibly, whether termination is clear and whether the terms link to a compliant privacy policy. These are the points buyers end up fighting over, and you have heard each one argued from the buyer's side.

Advising online marketplaces. Day 2 covers six ways tech companies make money, including marketplace and third-party seller models, and the 8 services e-commerce companies need, from structuring and policies to data protection and disputes.
