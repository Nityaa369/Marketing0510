01

You find the answer. Someone else uses it

You spend your day in judgments, bare acts and notifications. You know which amendment changed what, and you can find a precedent faster than most practising lawyers.

Then your research note goes up the chain. A lawyer argues it, a partner advises on it, an editor publishes it. Your name rarely appears, and the work you do best stays out of sight.

You enjoy the reading. You just want it to lead somewhere of your own.

02

Research that turns into tech law work

Tech law is the legal work tech companies need, and a lot of it is contracts, privacy, compliance and policy. Much of it begins with reading new rules closely and working out what they mean for one business. That is your daily work, pointed at a new kind of client.

Moving into tech law lets you keep the reading you are good at and add the step that makes it count. You turn the finding into a clause, a policy, a compliance note or a pitch, with your name on it.

04

Research is the first half of tech law work

Skill is not what you lack. You already have the half of tech law that many lawyers find hardest: patience with new rules and the habit of reading the whole text. The missing half is applying it to one company's business.

That second half can be learned. The camp shows how the legal work follows the business model, so you learn to ask how a company makes money, then which rules and documents follow from it. Your research instinct does the rest.

What staying behind the scenes costs

Research jobs are steady, and there is real value in them. The only cost is that your best work keeps carrying someone else's name. A few tech law documents of your own let you test a different path without giving up the one you have.

05

What tech law work looks like for a law graduate working as a legal researcher

Some of it is policy work. Think tanks and policy teams need research, white papers and replies to draft laws on tech. A researcher who already tracks notifications and amendments is halfway to a policy brief.

Some of it is structuring. A founder asks whether a business can be done, in which jurisdiction and at what cost. On Day 3 the camp walks through setting up a blockchain founder's venture as a Wyoming company, which is research applied to one client's question.

Some of it is review. A startup's terms are checked against six points, from liability caps to IP ownership, and the gaps are written up as a short pitch. That is a research note, rewritten for a founder who will act on it.
