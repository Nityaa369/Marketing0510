3 reasons you do not need a bank's offer letter to start

  1. Most early work is debt, and debt is everywhere.Working capital loans, term loans, SME loans, government schemes. Many owners could get a loan easily if someone guided them through the documents and the lenders. That guidance is corporate finance work, and it needs no desk at a bank.
  2. Deal experience comes from deals, not applications.Analyst postings ask for deal experience. Helping one small business get one loan sanctioned is a real deal you worked on, from the first meeting to the money arriving.
  3. People with capital listen to whoever brings deals.Investors and lenders ignore a beginner who brings nothing. A beginner with a business that needs money, and a clean file on it, is worth a meeting. So the camp teaches you to build the opportunity side first.

One raise, start to finish

How a single deal runs, in order. The business is a family-run packaging unit, the kind many of you know through a relative or a senior.

  1. The owner needs moneyThe unit wants a second machine and more stock for a bigger order. The owner knows the business, not how to raise money for it. You ask what the money is for, how much, and how soon.
  2. Choose a loan or investmentA loan is repaid with interest and the owner keeps the whole business. Investment means selling part of the ownership. For a machine and stock, a term loan plus working capital, money to run daily operations, usually fits better.
  3. Prepare the documentsFinancial projections, and a project report or loan proposal showing the use of funds and how the loan is repaid. An equity raise would need a pitch deck and an information memorandum, with valuation as one input when a stake is sold.
  4. Take it to lenders or investorsYou shortlist the banks, NBFCs (lenders that are not banks) and government schemes that fit, and put the file before the right relationship managers. These contacts are your capital funnel.
  5. Negotiate and closeThe lender offers terms: amount, rate, tenure, security. For investment it is a term sheet. You help the owner compare, push back where reasonable, and see the paperwork through until the money is paid out.
  6. How you are paidUsually a success fee on the loan sanctioned or money raised, paid at closing, normally priced at 1.5 to 3% for bank loans and 2 to 4% for equity. Plus a fixed fee for the documents, paid even if the deal does not close. Retainers come once you have a track record.

4 things an investment banking syllabus leaves out

  1. Debt deals, not only equity.Coursework centres on IPOs and acquisitions. Most early deals are loans: cash credit, term loans, invoice discounting, and revenue-based finance, a loan repaid out of monthly sales.
  2. Sell side and buy side.Sell side acts for the business raising money. Buy side acts for the lenders, investors or funds looking for businesses to fund. You can work for either.
  3. The process from pitch to closing.Market sizing with TAM, SAM and SOM, the pitch deck, the loan proposal, and a SAFE drafted clause by clause. A SAFE is where an investor pays now and gets shares in a later round.
  4. Where an IPO actually sits.A public issue is run by a SEBI-registered merchant banker. A beginner refers an IPO-sized business to one, for a referral commission.

How the first client reaches an aspiring investment banker

Not through a job portal. Through the people who already know you: the opportunity funnel.

  1. List the business owners who already trust youFamily businesses, a relative's unit, alumni founders, seniors running startups. The long-term goal is a hundred owners who would call you when they need money.
  2. Ask what they would do with more capitalMost owners have an unfunded plan. That plan tells you whether it needs a loan, a scheme or an investor.
  3. Offer the documents firstProjections and a loan proposal help the owner before any lender says yes. Preparing them for a fixed fee is often how a first client starts.
  4. Bring the file to the capital sideWith a real business and a clean file, a bank manager or merchant banker has a reason to meet you. That first closed deal is the experience an application asks for.
