What a cost accountant already does that a fundraise needs

Every business that raises money has to show a lender or an investor what its numbers will look like after the money arrives. Those numbers start with cost, margin and cash, which is the work you already do every month for one employer.

In your costing work now
The same skill on a corporate finance deal
Splitting cost into fixed, variable and the semi-variable middle
Building the projections a lender or investor reads before saying yes
Setting standard costs and budgets before the year starts
Writing the forecast inside a loan proposal or a pitch deck, and defending it
Working out how much stock and customer credit the business carries
Sizing the working capital loan the business actually needs, overdraft or cash credit
Explaining a variance to management in plain words
Explaining the numbers to a bank manager or an investor across the table
Pricing a product so the margin survives at low volume
Showing a lender the margin that repays the loan, even in a slow year

You already produce the numbers a raise runs on. What is new is taking those numbers to the people who have the money, and seeing the deal through to closing.

3 reasons a cost accountant is closer to corporate finance than most

  1. Most early corporate finance work is debt, and debt runs on cost and cash.Term loans and working capital limits are sanctioned on projections and repayment capacity. Many owners could get a loan easily if somebody guided them, using figures you work with daily.
  2. A lender questions the forecast first, and you know where forecasts break.You have seen a margin shrink below capacity and a standard miss its mark. A projection written with that knowledge holds up under a bank's questions.
  3. Business owners already trust a costing professional with their real numbers.Owners show you figures they publish nowhere else. A raise needs exactly that information, and a person trusted with it.

One real raise, start to finish, for a small manufacturing business

Take the kind of owner you already meet: a manufacturer who needs money to add a second production line. Here is the work, in the order it happens.

  1. The owner explains what the money is forMachinery, plus more stock and customer credit once it runs. You turn that into an amount and a date.
  2. You choose between a loan and investmentDebt is a loan the business repays. Equity is selling part of the ownership. For a profitable plant, a term loan plus a working capital limit is often the right mix.
  3. You prepare the documents the raise runs onProjections, a project report and a loan proposal. For investors, a pitch deck and an information memorandum, a full written description of the business. Valuation is one skill used here, when an equity price is agreed.
  4. You take the file to lenders or investorsBanks, NBFCs (lenders that are not banks), or angel investors, each chosen for the kind of business they fund.
  5. You carry the negotiation through to closingA lender returns a sanction with conditions. An investor returns a term sheet, a short document setting out price and terms. You keep it moving until the money arrives.
  6. You are paid on what closes, and for the documentsThe usual structure is a success fee on the loan sanctioned or the money raised, commonly 1.5 to 3% on bank loans and 2 to 4% on equity, plus fixed fees for the projections, project report and deck, paid even if the deal does not close. Retainers follow once you have a track record.

4 things a cost accountant has not been shown yet

  1. The full map of where business money comes from. Overdraft, cash credit, term loans, revenue-based finance (a loan repaid out of monthly sales), invoice discounting, government schemes, then angel, VC and PE rounds.
  2. How a pitch deck is built. The narrative, the data, the design and the story an investor follows, plus market sizing with TAM, SAM and SOM, the whole market down to the share one business can win.
  3. How to reach the people with capital. Bank relationship managers, NBFCs, funds, family offices and merchant bankers. People with money ignore a beginner who brings no deals, so the camp shows why business owners come first.
  4. How a deal closes on paper. Fundraising instruments, loan proposals, and a SAFE, a simple agreement an early investor signs for future shares, drafted clause by clause on the last evening along with choosing a deal structure.

How the first corporate finance client arrives for a cost accountant

Through the business owners who already know your work. The camp calls this your opportunity funnel: owners and SMEs who need a loan or investment and trust you enough to ask.

  1. List the owners whose numbers you have already seenSuppliers, customers, past employers. Each raises money at some point.
  2. Offer the part you are already good atStart with the projections and the project report for one owner's loan. That document carries a fixed fee in its own right, and it is the core of every raise.
  3. Take one file all the way to sanctionGo with the owner to the lender and answer the questions on the numbers. One closed deal is what lenders and owners remember.
  4. Build your capital funnel from that first dealThe bank manager who sanctioned it now knows you bring clean files. Owners who hear about it ask for the same help, and some of them will need investors, not only loans.

I have only worked in costing inside one company. Is that enough to start?

Yes. The projections, working capital and margins a raise depends on are costing work. The camp covers the parts that are new: the kinds of loans and investment, the documents, who to approach and how a deal closes.

No. Managing a public issue, an IPO, is the work of a SEBI-registered merchant banker. The camp covers the work around a private raise and a business loan, and how to refer a deal that is ready for an IPO to a merchant banker.
