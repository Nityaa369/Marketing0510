One funding round, seen from both sides of the same company

A round reaches you as a sequence of documents with dates attached. A resolution to draft, a valuation report to attach, an agreement to vet, a register to update. By the time the first of those is written, every decision that moved value has already been taken. Here is what was decided, and where you were standing when it happened.

What reaches your desk
What was settled before it got there
The valuation report that has to be attached to the allotment filing
What the company was said to be worth, argued out of a model, weeks earlier
The shareholders agreement, to be vetted, executed and kept
Liquidation preference, anti-dilution and who controls the board, fixed in the term sheet
The board and member resolutions approving the issue
How much was raised, at what price per share, and how much of the company the founders handed over to raise it
The option grant letters and the updated register of members
The size of the pool, and whether it was carved out before the money or after, which decides who actually paid for it
The filings, on a statutory deadline, charged per filing
The advice, over several weeks, charged for the engagement

Nothing in the left column is optional and nothing in it is where the value moved. You were in the room for both halves of that round. You were engaged for one.

3 reasons a company secretary is already sitting in the right chair

  1. You are the only person who sees every document in the round.The lawyer sees the agreement. The accountant sees the numbers. The investor sees the term sheet and nothing after it. You see all three, plus the register they eventually land in, which means you already hold the full picture of a deal.
  2. You know what a document can and cannot actually do.Most people negotiating a round have a rough idea of what they agreed and a much rougher idea of what the paperwork does with it. Knowing the difference is a large part of what a deal adviser is paid to notice.
  3. Founders tell you things they put nowhere else.A promise made to an early employee, a family arrangement, a note that was never properly papered. Every one of those sits in the cap table and surfaces at the next round, and you are usually the only person who knows it is there.

The round, in the order the decisions are actually taken

Six steps. The documents you own today appear at step five.

  1. The company decides what it needs, and argues for itHow much money, to fund how many months of operations, against what plan. The amount is an output of the plan rather than a wish, and it is settled long before an investor is approached.
  2. Somebody puts a value on the companyOn discounted cash flows where there is a history to discount, on multiples of comparable businesses, or on whatever the round itself implies. Every later number in the deal is derived from this one.
  3. The price and the instrument are negotiatedEquity now at a fixed price, or a SAFE that converts later at a cap and a discount. Two very different outcomes for the founders, decided in conversation, never in a form.
  4. The term sheet fixes who controls whatBoard composition, reserved matters, liquidation preference, anti-dilution. One clause here moves more value than every filing in the round put together.
  5. The cap table is redrawnBefore the money, after the money, and again after the option pool. Whoever builds that table is the only person who can show a founder what each version of the deal costs them.
  6. The documents are drafted, approved and filedThis is the part you own now. It is accurate, it is time bound, it is necessary, and it is last.

Two ways to add the deal side without abandoning the practice

Route one

In house, further up the table

A business that raises money more than once needs somebody inside who can maintain the cap table, model the next round and read a term sheet without sending it out for a view. That is a different brief from secretarial compliance, in the same organisation.

Route two

In practice, as a deal adviser

A practice already doing secretarial work for private companies can offer valuation and deal documentation to those same clients. The relationship exists. What is missing is the ability to price the deal rather than paper it.

Both routes need the same three things: a valuation you can defend, a cap table you can build, and a term sheet you can read clause by clause.

How the first deal advisory client arrives

Out of the registers you already maintain, not out of an advertisement.

  1. Go back through the companies on your own listMark every one that has taken outside money, granted options, admitted a new partner or talked about being bought. Each of those events had a valuation behind it, and almost none of those valuations were yours.
  2. Rebuild one of those cap tables the way an adviser wouldTake a round you filed and redo it properly: the table before the money and after it, the pool, and the dilution at a second round. Then show the founder what they hold now and what the next round does to it.
  3. Offer the next round as an engagement, not as a filingOne sentence. Before the papers are drafted, you will build the cap table and read the term sheet against it. Name the deliverable and name the date it arrives.
  4. Keep the model alive after the round closesA founder who has watched their own cap table modelled comes back at the next round, and mentions it to the other founders they see every month.

Is this the same as becoming a registered valuer?

No. Registered valuation is a separate registration with its own examination, and certain filings require a report from a registered valuer. This camp teaches the analytical work itself: the model, the two valuation methods, the term sheet, the SAFE and the cap table. It is not a registration and does not stand in for one.

Do I need a CA or a CFA?

No. The camp assumes you can read a profit and loss account and a balance sheet. Everything past that, including the valuation and the model, is built in front of you.

No. The clients are the same clients and the round is the same round. What changes is which part of it you are engaged for, and when in the sequence you are brought in.
