What an equity research analyst already does, on a real fundraise

Research writes about companies after the money is raised. Corporate finance works on the raise itself, and most of that work you already do every quarter.

In equity research today
On a corporate finance deal
You build a forecast from a company's statements and defend every assumption
You build the financial projections a lender or investor reads before saying yes
You write an initiation report that argues a case for a business
You write the pitch deck or information memorandum that argues the case for funding it
You size a sector and explain where the growth sits
You size the market for a pitch as TAM, SAM and SOM: the whole market, the part served, the part winnable
You pitch your ideas to fund managers who allocate money
You take a deal to investors, funds, family offices and bank relationship managers who fund businesses
You question management on calls and in meetings
You sit with the owner or CFO and stay with the deal until it closes

The skills carry straight over. What changes is that you work on the money going in, not on the stock afterwards.

3 reasons equity research analysts start ahead in corporate finance

  1. You already write the documents a raise runs on.A raise needs projections, a pitch deck and an information memorandum, a detailed document describing the business for investors. Each is an argument for a company, backed by numbers: your report, pointed at a funding decision instead of a rating.
  2. You already know the people who hold capital.Corporate finance needs two sets of relationships: businesses that need money, and the investors, lenders, funds and family offices who have it. Research already connects you to fund managers and the buy side, the half beginners find hardest to reach.
  3. You can read a business quickly and honestly.Lenders trust a file from someone who has tested the numbers. Checking a forecast against what the business can deliver is what makes a funding proposal credible.

One raise, from the owner's first call to the fee

The order a typical fundraise happens in. Count the steps you already do.

  1. The owner needs moneyA manufacturer wants more capacity, or a startup needs to fund its next year. You work out how much is needed, for what, and by when. Acting for the business is called the sell side.
  2. Choose a loan or investmentDebt means a loan the business repays, such as a term loan, a working capital limit or revenue-based finance, which is a loan repaid out of monthly sales. Equity means selling part of the ownership to an angel, a VC or a PE fund. Debt is most of the early work.
  3. Prepare the documentsFinancial projections, then a loan proposal or project report for a lender, or a pitch deck and information memorandum for investors. Valuation is one skill used here, when an investor asks what share their money buys.
  4. Take it to lenders or investorsThe file goes to the banks, NBFCs, angels, funds or family offices that fund this kind of business.
  5. Negotiate and closeAn investor sends a term sheet, the terms they propose. Early startups often use a SAFE, an agreement that turns the investment into shares later. You carry it until the money arrives or the loan is sanctioned.
  6. How you are paidMostly a success fee, a percentage of the money raised or the loan sanctioned, paid when the deal closes, usually 2 to 4% on equity and 1.5 to 3% on bank loans. Documents carry fixed fees paid even if the deal does not close. Retainers follow a track record.

How the first fundraising client reaches an equity research analyst

Capital follows deals. Investors ignore a beginner who brings them nothing, so the first job is to build the opportunity side: business owners who trust you.

  1. Start with the companies you already understandSuppliers, distributors and unlisted peers of the companies you cover, in sectors you know are growing, are natural first names.
  2. Offer the document firstMany owners need projections or a loan proposal first. Preparing it is paid work, and it puts you inside the business when the raise begins.
  3. Bring the deal to the capital side you knowTake the ready file to the lenders, funds and investors you meet through research.
  4. Refer what is too big, and follow a written planAn IPO is run by a SEBI-registered merchant banker, so an IPO-sized deal is referred to one for a referral commission. Day 3 ends with a written 6-month plan to your first mandate.
