What a CA in practice already does, and where it leads

You already prepare the accounts a lender reads, the CMA data for a working capital limit and the project report behind a term loan. Corporate finance is the rest of that deal: finding the money and staying on until it closes.

The right hand column needs no new clients. It needs the products, the documents and the people with the money.

3 reasons a CA practice is a strong place to start

  1. You already have the opportunity funnel.Corporate finance runs on two sets of relationships: business owners who need money, and the lenders and investors who have it. A practice already has the first.
  2. Owners tell you the facts a lender will ask about.The customer who pays late, the loan from a relative, the year that was weak. You heard it last March, so your file is honest from the first draft.
  3. Debt is most of the early work, and you already speak it.Working capital limits, term loans and secured SME loans are the first deals most people do. The camp adds the equity side.

One raise for a practice client, start to finish

A manufacturing client whose returns you file wants to add a second production line. This is the order the work happens in.

  1. The owner tells you what they needMore capacity, a bigger order book, and not enough cash to pay for either.
  2. You decide between a loan and investmentA term loan for the machines and a working capital limit for the stock is often the right answer. If the plan is larger than a lender will fund, you look at selling a minority share to an investor instead.
  3. You build the documentsFinancial projections for five years, the loan proposal or project report, and, on the equity route, a pitch deck and an information memorandum.
  4. You take the file to lenders or investorsBank relationship managers, NBFCs, a government scheme that suits the sector, or angel and family office investors.
  5. You carry the negotiation to closingRate, security, covenants and the repayment schedule on a loan. On investment, the term sheet: how much, for what share, and what rights the investor gets. Valuing the business is one input here, not the whole job. You stay until the loan is sanctioned or the money arrives.
  6. You are paid on what closesThe usual structure is a success fee on the money raised or the loan sanctioned, commonly 1.5 to 3 percent on bank loans and 2 to 4 percent on equity, plus fixed fees for the documents, which are paid even if the deal does not close.

4 things a CA in practice has not been taught about raising money

  1. The full menu of debt.Beyond cash credit and term loans: revenue-based financing, a loan repaid out of monthly sales, inventory-backed finance, project finance, and government schemes and grants.
  2. How an equity raise runs.Angel, VC and PE rounds, the pitch deck and its story, market sizing with TAM, SAM and SOM, and the process from first pitch to closing.
  3. What a SAFE is, clause by clause.A simple agreement for future equity: money now, shares later, at a price set by a future round. You draft one on the last evening.
  4. Where the capital actually sits.Which lenders, funds, family offices and merchant bankers to approach, how to reach them, and why they ignore anyone who brings no deals.

How the first raise arrives for a CA in practice

From the opportunity funnel your practice already has.

  1. List the clients who have asked about money this yearThe ones who asked for a CMA, mentioned a bank limit, wanted to expand, or said an investor had called. Each is a possible raise.
  2. Tell the owners you do not audit what you now doOne sentence: you help businesses raise loans and investment and see the deal through. Tax clients, referrals and family businesses in the same trade are the first audience.
  3. Quote the raise as its own engagementA separate letter for the documents and a success fee on what closes, never folded into the annual retainer.
  4. Build the capital side one deal at a timeDeals too large for you, such as an IPO, go to a merchant banker for a referral commission.

Straight answers

Why is it only ₹10?

The fee is ₹10 including GST, and it is refundable at any time. It exists so that the people in the room have chosen to be there, not to price the training.

Can I raise money for a company I audit?

Section 144 of the Companies Act bars an auditor from providing investment banking or investment advisory services to a company it audits, or to its holding or subsidiary company. So this work goes to clients you do not audit: tax clients, referrals and other owners in your network. The skills taught are the same either way.

Do I need a CA or a CFA?

No. The camp assumes you can read a profit and loss account and a balance sheet. Everything past that, including the projections and the deal documents, is built in front of you.

I already make project reports. What is new?

The project report is one document. The camp covers the rest of the raise: which product fits, the equity route, the pitch deck, the term sheet and SAFE, finding lenders and investors, and how the engagement is priced.

Does this make me a merchant banker?

No. Managing a public issue, an IPO, is the work of a SEBI-registered merchant banker. The camp covers the work around a private raise and a business loan, and how to refer a deal that is ready for an IPO to a merchant banker.

What if I miss a session?

Sessions are live only, with no recordings, because your own questions get answered in the room. You keep the templates, the model and the notes, and the WhatsApp group helps you catch up.

Do you place me with a client or an employer?

No. Nobody can promise that. What you get is the work itself, the templates, and a written 6-month plan to your first client, which is the part most people never write.
