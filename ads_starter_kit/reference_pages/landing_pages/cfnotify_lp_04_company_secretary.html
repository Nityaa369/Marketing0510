What a company secretary already does, and the same skill on a fundraising deal

When a company borrows or takes in investment, the paperwork lands with you. The board resolution, the charge filing, the allotment. You see the raise at the end. Corporate finance is the part that comes first: finding the lender or investor, preparing their documents, and carrying the deal to closing.

What you do today as a company secretary

You draft the board resolution that approves a borrowing or a share issue
You help the owner decide how much to raise, and whether it should be a loan or investment
You file the charge when a bank sanctions a loan against the company's assets

3 reasons a company secretary is already halfway into corporate finance

  1. Business owners already trust you with their company.They call you before a board meeting and when a bank asks for a document. The camp calls this the opportunity funnel: owners who trust you and would take your advice on money.
  2. You already know what lenders and investors ask for on paper.Charge documents, borrowing resolutions and allotment papers have passed through your hands. Most people who try to arrange a raise have never seen closing paperwork.
  3. You work across many companies, not one.A practising company secretary serves many private companies and SMEs. Many need a loan every year. Each one is a possible mandate, a client who asks you to arrange the raise.

One raise for an SME client, start to finish

A manufacturing company you already file for wants to grow. Here is the deal in the order it actually happens.

  1. The owner says the business needs moneyA new machine or a bigger order book. You ask how much, for what, and how it will be repaid.
  2. You choose between a loan and investmentDebt is a loan the business repays. Equity is selling part of the ownership for investment. For most SMEs the answer is debt: a term loan, cash credit or a government scheme.
  3. You prepare the documentsFinancial projections, and a loan proposal or project report explaining the need and the repayment. For an investment round, a pitch deck instead.
  4. You take the file to lenders or investorsBank relationship managers, NBFCs, which are finance companies that lend like banks, or angel investors. This is the capital funnel.
  5. You carry the negotiation through to closingThe bank's sanction letter or the investor's term sheet. You compare terms and see it through to the money arriving. The filings at the end you already know.
  6. You are paid on what closesThe usual structure is a success fee, a percentage of the money raised or the loan sanctioned, paid on closing: often 1.5 to 3% for bank loans and 2 to 4% for equity. The documents carry a fixed fee even if the deal does not close.

4 things a company secretary does not yet do on a deal, taught in the camp

  1. Which kind of money fits which business.Working capital, term loans, invoice discounting, which is a loan against unpaid customer bills, and government schemes. Then angel, VC and PE rounds for businesses ready for equity.
  2. How to build the pitch deck and projections.Market sizing with TAM, SAM and SOM, the market a business can realistically reach, then the story and numbers an investor reads.
  3. How the fundraising process runs from pitch to closing.The instruments, the loan proposal, the information memorandum, and a SAFE drafted clause by clause. A SAFE is an agreement where the investor pays now and receives shares at a later round. Valuation is covered as one skill inside this, with one real case.
  4. How to reach lenders, investors and first clients.Finding CFOs and business owners to approach, reaching investment banks and VCs to offer your services, and when to refer an IPO-sized deal to a SEBI-registered merchant banker.

How a company secretary finds the first fundraising client

Start with the companies already on your list. The camp calls this building the opportunity funnel first, because lenders and investors ignore a beginner who brings them no deals.

  1. Go through your own client listMark every company that has borrowed, created a charge or allotted shares in the last two years. Those owners will need money again.
  2. Ask one owner what the business needs nextAt the next board meeting, ask: are you planning to borrow or raise money this year?
  3. Offer the first piece of work as a documentProjections or a loan proposal, for a fixed fee. It is useful to the owner whether or not a lender says yes.
  4. Take that file to two or three lendersA bank relationship manager, an NBFC, or a government scheme where one fits. The first sanction becomes your track record.

Yes. Treasury and the CFO office do the same work inside one company: raising debt or equity, and the projections behind it.
