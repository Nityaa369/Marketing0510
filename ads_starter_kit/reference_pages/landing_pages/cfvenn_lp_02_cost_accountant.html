What a cost sheet measures, and what a valuation measures

Both are the same act of measurement. A cost sheet takes one product apart until every rupee inside it has a name. A valuation takes a whole company apart the same way. What changes is the unit being measured and the period it is measured over.

On a cost sheet
In a valuation of the same firm
The unit is a product, a process or a batch
The unit is the firm, every line added together and netted off
The period is the month that just closed
The period is the years ahead, forecast and then discounted back
A variance is measured against a standard you set yourself
A number is defended against a buyer who set none of it and agrees with none of it
Overheads are absorbed, allocated and explained
Overheads sit inside the margin that the multiple is applied to
The output is a report management reads and files
The output is a range that somebody signs a cheque against

Nobody has to explain absorption or contribution to you. What nobody has shown you is the firm itself treated as the unit, and the future treated as the period.

3 reasons a costing professional is nearer to valuation than most people in the room

  1. You know which margins are real and which ones vanish at low volume.A valuation lives or dies on its margin assumptions. Almost everybody who builds one has only ever seen a margin as a line in a published account. You have seen it move when the line runs below capacity, and that is the assumption a buyer attacks first.
  2. A standard cost is a forecast with a different name.Setting a standard means committing to what a thing should cost before it has been made, then answering for the difference. That is exactly the discipline a financial model asks for, applied to a longer horizon.
  3. You are already trusted with the numbers a company does not publish.Nobody outside the business sees the process cost or the true capacity. Pricing a private company depends on that kind of information, and on somebody who can be shown it.

One valuation, built upward from the cost structure

A valuation does not start with a discount rate. It starts with the split you already make every month.

  1. Split the spending into what moves with volume and what does notVariable, fixed, and the semi-variable middle that gets argued about. Every forecast that follows rests on this split, and getting it wrong is the commonest reason a model falls over under questioning.
  2. Turn the split into a forecast the business can challengeRevenue by line, contribution by line, fixed cost held flat until a capacity argument forces it to step up. Each assumption written where the owner can push back on it.
  3. Convert the profit into cashWorking capital, capital expenditure, tax. A discounted cash flow has no interest in profit. It wants the cash that is actually free after the business has been kept running.
  4. Discount it, and watch what the rate doesThe discount rate is the assumption people fight hardest over. You see the value move as it changes, which is how you learn which parts of a valuation are solid and which parts are opinion wearing a decimal point.
  5. Price the same company a second way, on multiplesComparable businesses, and what they have changed hands for. Two independent methods, so the discounted cash flow is not the only witness in the room.
  6. Write the range, and the reasons for itThe reasons are the deliverable. A number with no written reasoning behind it is a number the other side rewrites for you.

Two places this work goes

Route one

Inside the company you already work in

The firm that buys a competitor, leases a second line or takes in an investor needs somebody who can price the thing. Today that analysis is usually bought outside while the person who knows the operation best is costing the product it makes.

Route two

As an adviser to owners of other businesses

Owners of manufacturing and process businesses need a defensible number when a partner leaves, a buyer appears or a lender asks what the business is worth. That is engagement work, scoped and delivered, for people who already trust a costing professional.

The analysis is identical on both routes. The three days build it once, and the written plan on the last evening is about whichever route you pick.

How the first corporate finance client arrives for a cost accountant

Through the factory floor, which is where you already stand and where almost no valuation adviser has ever been.

  1. List every business whose plant you have actually walked throughSuppliers, customers, the firms your own employer benchmarks against. Owners of those businesses face a valuation question sooner or later, and they do not know who to ask.
  2. Lead with the one thing only you can sayA valuation written by somebody who has stood on the shop floor reads differently from one assembled out of a spreadsheet in another city. Say that plainly, in a sentence.
  3. Deliver one small engagement completelyOne company, valued two ways, with the model attached and a date on the cover. That document is what the next five conversations are built on, so it has to be finished.
  4. Ask the owner who else has been asked the same questionAn owner who has just seen their own business priced usually knows two more owners who were asked what theirs is worth and had no answer ready.

Straight answers

Why is it only ₹10?

The fee is ₹10 including GST, and it is refundable at any time. It exists so that the people in the room have chosen to be there, not to price the training.

Is this only useful if I work in manufacturing?

No. The costing habit of taking a number apart until every piece of it is explained is what transfers, and it transfers from any industry. Manufacturing simply gives you a warmer first list of businesses to approach.

Do I need a CA or a CFA?

No. The camp assumes you can read a profit and loss account and a balance sheet. Everything past that, including the valuation and the model, is built in front of you.

I work in costing software, not in financial models. Is that a problem?

No. The model is built in front of you on the second evening, from a blank sheet, with every formula explained as it goes in. You keep the file that gets built.

Will anyone take a valuation seriously from a costing background?

That depends on the work, not on the letters. A valuation is judged on whether the assumptions hold up when they are questioned, which is why the camp spends its time on the assumptions and the written reasoning rather than on the arithmetic.

What if I miss a session?

Sessions are live only, with no recordings, because your own questions get answered in the room. You keep the templates, the model and the notes, and the WhatsApp group helps you catch up.

Do you place me with a client or an employer?

No. Nobody can promise that. What you get is the work itself, the templates, and a written 6-month plan to your first client, which is the part most people never write.

Does this let me sign a valuation report?

No. A valuation the law requires, filed under company law or with a tax authority, is signed by a registered valuer, and a discounted cash flow valuation of unquoted shares for income tax comes from a SEBI-registered merchant banker. What you learn here is the analytical work behind a decision. Where a signed statutory report is needed, you work with the person entitled to sign it.
