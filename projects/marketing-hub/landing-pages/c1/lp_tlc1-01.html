This page is for you if you hold an LLB, work in HR, and spend your days with offer letters, policies and vendor terms that someone else signs off.

You read the contracts. Someone else gets to be the lawyer.

Your desk sees every offer letter, every leave policy and every vendor agreement for the payroll or attendance software. You notice when a clause is vague. Then you forward it to the legal team and wait.

Somewhere along the way, people stopped remembering you have a law degree. You may have stopped mentioning it yourself. It feels wasted in HR, and you have said so more than once.

From the person who forwards the contract to the person who drafts it

Tech law covers what technology companies need from a lawyer, from contracts and privacy to compliance and disputes. A large part of it is reading and drafting documents very like the ones already on your desk. The difference is that here, the reading is the job, and you are hired for it.

A move into tech law could take you into drafting for startups, into a firm's technology team, or into the legal side of a tech company. The craft is one you already practise. The payer and the title change.

Your HR years are the asset, not the detour

You did not lose your law degree in HR. You kept using its main skill, close reading, on employment and vendor documents every day. The issue is how you are positioned, not how hard you have worked.

Think about what HR buys. Payroll tools, attendance apps and hiring platforms are SaaS products sold under subscription terms. You have been the customer on the other side of those terms for years, and tech companies need lawyers who know how customers really read them.

What tech law work looks like for an LLB holder in HR

IP clauses are a good starting point. In a software development agreement, the developer assigns the IP in the work to the client and hands over the code. You already know why ownership of work matters from offer letters, and the tech version follows the same logic.

Privacy is the second area. Tech companies hold personal data about users and staff, and every one of them needs a privacy policy that tells the truth about it. You have handled employee records under confidentiality rules, which is the practical side of the same work.

Third, SaaS contracts. On Day 1 the camp explains SaaS, SLAs and white labelling. Those are the terms inside the HR software deals you have seen from the buyer's side, now drafted from the seller's side.
